CJC-1295 and Ipamorelin are two different research peptides supplied together in one vial for laboratory use. Because a single vial holds two compounds, one purity figure cannot describe both of them. A useful certificate names which CJC-1295 variant is present, reports each component separately, and ties those results to the lot being shipped.

## One number cannot describe two peptides

Start with the arithmetic, because it drives everything else.

**The following is a hypothetical illustration. It is not a real certificate, not a real lot, and not any supplier's document.** Picture a vial sold as a 10 mg blend of two peptides. A laboratory tests the contents and reports 99% purity. That figure says something narrower than it first appears. It says that 99% of what the instrument detected was peptide material rather than salts, solvents or synthesis byproducts.

It does not say the vial held the two peptides in the proportion you ordered. Both of the components in that vial are peptides. A vial weighted heavily toward one of them can still test at 99% purity, because the purity measurement is looking for contamination, not for balance. The number stays high while the composition drifts.

That gap is a structural property of any multi-component material. It is not an accusation against any supplier, and it does not disappear by choosing a better one. It only closes when the documentation reports each component on its own.

So the question for a wholesale buyer is not whether a purity figure looks good. The question is what the certificate has to say before that figure means anything at all.

## What a purity percentage actually measures

Buyers tend to collapse several different things into the word purity. Pulling them apart is the fastest way to read a certificate properly.

**Identity** answers which molecule is present. **Purity** answers what proportion of the detected material was the intended peptide. **Content** answers how much peptide is actually in the vial by mass. **Composition** answers which components are present in a mixture. **Ratio** answers in what proportion they appear. **Traceability** answers whether the result belongs to the vial you will receive. **Method** answers how any of it was determined.

A single percentage speaks to one of those seven. Treating it as though it speaks to all of them is the most common reading error in peptide procurement.

Two practical consequences follow from that distinction. A purity figure carrying no lot number and no test date is not information you can act on, because nothing connects it to your material. And a high figure establishes nothing about sterility, endotoxin levels, water content or residual solvents, because the method was not looking for them. Absence of evidence on a certificate is not evidence of absence.

## The variant has to be named on the certificate, not just the label

Here is where blend documentation gets genuinely difficult, and where most buyers stop asking questions too early.

CJC-1295 is sold in two forms. One carries a modification that binds it to circulating albumin, and the other does not. These are different molecules with meaningfully different masses, and they are not interchangeable stock. A research program built around one of them is not served by the other.

The complication is that both forms circulate in public chemical listings under the same compound name. Search the registry layer and you will find entries labeled simply CJC-1295, with suppliers attaching different molecular formulas to overlapping identifiers. Some listings describe a structural modification without ever using the terms DAC or no-DAC. The identifiers do not reliably sort the two molecules apart.

We will not tell you which registry number belongs to which variant. The public sources conflict on that point. We could not resolve it against a primary record. That uncertainty is the finding, not a gap in the research. If the registry layer cannot settle variant identity, a buyer should never rely on it to do so.

Documentation is what settles the question instead. The certificate should name the variant explicitly. It should also support that name with a stated sequence and a measured mass for the lot in hand. Mass is the discriminating measurement here, because the two forms differ by a substantial margin.

The distinction in one line: a label naming a variant is a claim, and a certificate naming it with supporting data is a finding. Buyers pay for the second one and too often accept the first.

Worth knowing is that naming the variant is already standard practice elsewhere in this market. Browse retail listings for this compound pair and you will routinely see the no-DAC designation written into the product name. Asking a wholesale supplier to carry that same clarity onto the certificate is not an unusual demand.

The labeled against measured line deserves particular attention, because it is the only field that checks the fill rather than the chemistry. Purity tells you about quality of material. A measured content figure tells you whether the vial holds what the label promises. Those are different failures, and a certificate reporting only the first leaves the second entirely unexamined.

Component reporting is the other field worth insisting on. Some certificates for blended material report one combined figure for the whole vial. Others report each peptide separately, with its own identity confirmation and its own result. Both formats exist in the market today for this exact compound pair. The second format answers a question the first cannot. A buyer who does not specify which one they need will sometimes receive the weaker version.

On ratio, be direct with your supplier. If the formulation ratio is not documented anywhere, you cannot confirm it, and you should not assume it from the product name. Ask whether the ratio is documented, and ask what the documentation shows. A supplier who can answer that question has thought about blend quality control. A supplier who deflects it to the quote stage has told you something too.

## Eight questions to ask before you approve a blend supplier

Work through these questions during supplier evaluation. Each one includes what to request and what a good answer does not cover, because knowing the limits matters as much as getting the answer.

**1. Can you provide a lot-specific certificate for the exact lot being quoted?**
Request the certificate carrying the lot number that will appear on the vials. A satisfactory answer connects documentation to material. It does not establish that the material is suitable, only that the paperwork refers to it. A generic example certificate answers a different question entirely, and should be treated as marketing rather than documentation.

**2. Does the documentation analyze each component separately?**
Ask for per-component identity and purity results. A satisfactory answer tells you both peptides were found and measured individually. It does not tell you the proportion between them unless content figures are reported too. If only a combined figure exists, ask directly whether component-level testing is available.

**3. Which laboratory performed the testing, and can the report be authenticated?**
Get the laboratory name, contact details and any report identifier. A satisfactory answer gives you a route to verify the document independently. It does not establish independence on its own. A supplier describing a laboratory as third-party is making a claim, not providing proof, and the same laboratories often serve multiple competing suppliers.

**4. Which CJC-1295 variant is in this product, and what confirms it?**
Request the stated sequence and the measured mass. A satisfactory answer lets you check the variant against a reference. It does not help if the certificate names the variant without supporting data. Follow up by asking what the measured mass was, not merely which variant the label says.

**5. What peptide content is declared per vial for each component, and how was it determined?**
Ask for both the declared figures and the method used. A satisfactory answer distinguishes nominal formulation from measured quantity. It does not confirm the ratio unless both components were measured. Watch for content and purity being quoted as though they were the same measurement.

**6. What testing addresses contamination beyond purity?**
Find out which of endotoxin, residual solvents, heavy metals and water content were actually tested, and which were not. A satisfactory answer tells you the scope of what is known. It does not mean untested parameters are acceptable or unacceptable, only undocumented. Different research applications justify different panels, so the useful question is what applies to yours rather than demanding a universal list.

**7. What research-use documentation accompanies the purchase?**
Request the actual attestation or agreement text. A satisfactory answer shows what responsibilities the arrangement places on you as the buyer. It does not establish that any particular transaction is lawful in your jurisdiction. An attestation is a term of sale, not a legal determination, and should never be read as one.

**8. What are the confirmed purchasing terms for this product?**
Get the minimum order quantity, lead time, shipping origin and conditions, and replacement terms in writing. A satisfactory answer gives you terms you can plan against. It does not bind anything until it appears in a quote or agreement. Published policy and transaction-specific terms are different documents, and buyers are sometimes quoted against one while planning around the other.

Working through all eight does not guarantee product quality or regulatory compliance. No checklist of questions can do that. What it does is replace assumption with documentation, which is the only part of supplier evaluation a buyer actually controls.

Neither variant is superior to the other. A research requirement decides which one is appropriate. If your requirement does not specify, that is a question for your protocol rather than for your supplier. Where the requirement is unclear, the missing information is usually the intended duration of exposure in the experimental model, not anything a supplier can provide.

One consequence is easy to miss. A premixed blend makes the variant decision for you. Whoever formulated it chose, and your documentation either records that choice or it does not. If your program might need the other variant later, buying premixed locks in a decision you did not make.

## What the research actually shows and where it stops

Evidence for these compounds is frequently overstated in commercial material. Here is what the record supports, with the category of evidence named.

**Human evidence for CJC-1295 with DAC.** A randomized, placebo-controlled study appeared in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism in 2006. It examined CJC-1295 with DAC in healthy subjects aged 21 to 61. Reported increases were 2 to 10 fold for growth hormone and 1.5 to 3 fold for IGF-I. This study examined the DAC-containing form alone. It was not a study of the blend, and it says nothing about the no-DAC variant.

**Animal and in vitro evidence, Ipamorelin.** A 1998 paper in the European Journal of Endocrinology characterized Ipamorelin as a selective growth hormone secretagogue. The work used rat pituitary cells, anesthetized rats and conscious swine. All of that work is preclinical rather than clinical. It is not human evidence, and findings in these models do not transfer to human outcomes.

**Evidence for the two compounds combined.** We identified no published human trial of the CJC-1295 and Ipamorelin combination. A mechanistic rationale for combining them exists, and a rationale is not a result. Any claim that the blend produces a particular outcome in humans is unsupported by the published record.

**Why this matters for procurement teams.** A 2026 narrative review in Sexual Medicine Reviews looked at this compound class. It noted that secretagogues reliably raise growth hormone and IGF-1 concentrations. Evidence for functional benefit remains limited. The same review observed that CJC-1295 and related compounds remain accessible through unregulated markets, raising concerns about product quality and variability. That is a peer-reviewed source identifying material quality as a live problem in this compound class. Component-level documentation is worth the effort for exactly that reason.
